Turn around

The scene

A world made of shadows

Around 375 BC, in the seventh book of the Republic, Plato has his teacher Socrates ask his brother Glaucon to picture a strange scene. Deep underground, people sit in chains, facing a wall. They have been there since childhood and cannot turn their heads.

Behind them a fire burns. Between the fire and the prisoners runs a raised path with a low wall, like the screen of a puppet show. People walk along it carrying statues and figures of animals made of wood and stone, holding them up above the wall. The fire throws their shadows onto the wall in front of the prisoners. When the carriers talk, the echo seems to come from the shadows.

“You have shown me a strange image, and they are strange prisoners.” “Like ourselves,” I replied.Republic 515a

Shadows are all the prisoners have ever seen, so to them the shadows are reality. They even hold contests and give prizes to whoever is quickest at naming the shadows and best at guessing which one will come next.

  2. 2The prisonersChained by legs and neck since childhood. Plato says they are like us.
  3. 3The low wall and the carriersPeople hold up statues and figures, like puppeteers behind a screen.
  4. 4The fireThe light inside the cave, which makes every shadow possible.
  5. 5The steep ascentA long, rough passage up to the mouth of the cave and the daylight.
  6. 6The world outsideShadows, then reflections in water, then the real things themselves.

The journey

The climb to the light

Now, says Socrates, imagine one prisoner is set free. Freedom does not feel like freedom at first. Every step of the way out hurts, and at every step he would rather go back.

  1. 1

    Chained

    He knows nothing but the shadows and has no reason to doubt them. Nothing feels missing.

    “To them, I said, the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images.”515c
  2. 2

    Turned around

    He is made to stand, turn and look at the fire. The light hurts his eyes. When he is shown the real objects and told they are more real than the shadows, he is confused and wants to turn back to what he knows.

    “Will he not fancy that the shadows which he formerly saw are truer than the objects which are now shown to him?”515d
  3. 3

    Dragged up

    He is pulled up the steep, rough passage against his will. He is in pain and angry, and when he comes out into the sunlight he is so dazzled that he cannot see anything at all.

    “He is reluctantly dragged up a steep and rugged ascent, and held fast until he is forced into the presence of the sun himself.”515e
  4. 4

    Getting used to the light

    Slowly his eyes adjust. First he can look at shadows, then at reflections of people and things in water, then at the things themselves. At night he looks at the moon and the stars, which is easier than facing the sun by day.

    “He will require to grow accustomed to the sight of the upper world.”516a
  5. 5

    Looking at the sun

    At last he can look at the sun itself, not at its reflection, and understand it: the sun makes the seasons and the years, governs everything that can be seen, and is in a way the cause of everything he used to see in the cave.

  6. 6

    Pity for the cave

    He remembers the contests over the shadows and the honours given to the best guessers. He would not trade places with the winners. He feels sorry for those still below.

    “Better to be the poor servant of a poor master, and to endure anything, rather than think as they do and live after their manner.”516d, Socrates quoting Homer

516b

The sun itself

“He will see the sun, and not mere reflections of him in the water, but he will see him in his own proper place, and not in another; and he will contemplate him as he is.”

For Plato the sun is the Form of the Good: the highest thing there is to understand. Just as the sun lets us see and lets things grow, the Good is what makes truth knowable and gives everything its being.

The return

Going back down

Now imagine the freed prisoner goes back to his old seat in the cave. Coming in from the sun, he is blind in the dark. Before his eyes adjust, he has to compete again at naming the shadows, and he is worse at it than everyone else.

Laughed at

The others conclude that going up ruined his eyes, and that the climb is not worth making.

Feared

If he tried to free them and lead them up, and they could get their hands on him, they would kill him.

Socrates

Plato's readers knew this ending. In 399 BC Athens put Socrates to death for questioning what everyone believed.

“Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes; and that it was better not even to think of ascending; and if any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death.”517a

The meaning

What the images stand for

Socrates explains the story himself. The cave is the world we know through our senses. The climb out is the mind rising to understanding.

“The prison-house is the world of sight, the light of the fire is the sun, and you will not misapprehend me if you interpret the journey upwards to be the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world.”517b
In the storyWhat it stands for
Shadows on the wallImages, appearances, opinions taken second-hand: what we take for real without ever questioning it.
The carried objectsThe physical things of everyday life, which the shadows only copy.
The fireThe sun of the visible world: the light that lets us see bodies, but not truth.
Things outsideThe Forms: the true, unchanging nature of things (Justice itself, Beauty itself), grasped by reason.
The sunThe Form of the Good, the source of truth, of knowing, and of being.
The chainsHabit, comfort, upbringing, and the fear of what lies beyond what we already believe.

Four kinds of seeing

Just before the cave, Plato draws a line divided into four parts, each a clearer way of knowing. The climb out of the cave walks up the same line.

eikasia
Imagining. Taking images and shadows for the real thing. In the story: the prisoners watching the wall.
pistis
Belief. Trusting the physical things we see and touch. In the story: turning to see the fire and the objects.
dianoia
Reasoning. Thinking in models and proofs, like mathematics. In the story: reflections and things in the daylight.
noēsis
Understanding. Seeing the Forms directly, and finally the Good. In the story: looking at the sun.

The divided line, Republic 509d–511e.

Education

Learning is turning around

Plato draws a surprising lesson about teaching. Education is not pouring knowledge into an empty mind, like putting sight into blind eyes. The power to see is already there. What a teacher can do is help someone turn around and face the right way.

“The power and capacity of learning exists in the soul already; and … just as the eye was unable to turn from darkness to light without the whole body, so too the instrument of knowledge can only by the movement of the whole soul be turned from the world of becoming into that of being.”518c

The Greek word for this is periagōgē, a turning around. It takes the whole person, not just the head, and it is slow: the eyes need time to adjust in both directions.

The duty to come back

Those who reach the light would rather stay there. Plato says they must not. In a just city, those who have seen the Good must go back down and help govern, precisely because they don't want power for its own sake.

“They must be made to descend again among the prisoners in the den, and partake of their labours and honours, whether they are worth having or not.”519d

Now

Our caves

Twenty-four centuries later the image still fits. We live surrounded by images of the world (screens, feeds, headlines, reputations) and it is easy to become an expert in shadows: quick to name them, rewarded for predicting them, never turning around.

The story also warns about the way out. Questioning what we believe is uncomfortable, and the first light feels worse than the familiar dark. And the one who has seen more should come back with patience, not contempt: his eyes, too, once had to adjust.

  • Which of your certainties have you only ever seen as shadows?
  • What holds you in your seat: chains, or comfort?
  • When you come back down, how will you speak to those still watching the wall?
